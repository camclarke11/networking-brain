# 15 — The Brand X-ray (pillar 2)

One of the account's two pillars (the other is the Move, `06-formats.md`): **how famous brands get people to buy, and how a young founder can steal the trick for their own business.** Same audience, same voice, a different engine from the Move. Format code **F7**. Every Brand X-ray is filed under one Playbook chapter (usually Price and package, Stand out, Sell or Get seen).

Reference account: **@decoyreport** (`examples/decoyreport/`, data in `data/decoyreport/`). From its first post on 13 October 2026 to 20 October: 23 posts, **median 70k views**, 14.1M total, with a **9.75M** breakout (the Tesla Roadster) and a **2.6M** (Apple's tilted laptops). Its transcripts are for structure only. Never copy a line, a sign-off or a caption.

What it means:
- **The viewer is the seller.** Every winner ends with a rule the viewer can use on their own customers. "So if you sell anything…" is the turn.
- **A brand everyone knows, doing something odd on purpose.** Tesla, Apple, Uber, Gucci. The oddness is the hook; the "on purpose" is the loop.
- **Short.** 100 to 155 words. One brand, one trick, one rule. Their scripts have no characters and no objection; ours put the viewer in the store and use a wrong guess instead (§2).
- Caveats: one export, all posts under 8 days old, and the breakout rode a news event (the Roadster reveal on 15 October 2026). Newsy brand moments plus a trick may be the real engine.

## 2. The skeleton (about 45 seconds, told as a scene)

The viewer is **in the store, holding the product**, then turns into the seller at the end. The odd thing is the first loop; each answer opens a deeper one; the full "why" lands in the last 20%. No citations: the facts are verified and told as what happens to you (`04-lesson-engine.md` §1).

| # | Beat | Opens | Shape (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead hook** (N12 drop-in, or N9 to N11 said as a scene): you, the odd thing, a pointer | Why would they do that? | "It's 2012. You walk into an Apple store. Every laptop is tilted to the exact same angle." |
| 2 | **Make it real:** one or two lines of specifics (who did it, how often, the number). Said as fact, never "X reported" | | "Somebody set every one of them like that before the doors opened." |
| 3 | **Wrong guess:** the obvious reason, knocked down | Then what is it for? | "You'd think it's for the glare. It isn't." |
| 4 | **The scene:** what you actually do, step by step, present tense | | "The angle bugs you, so you push the screen back." |
| 5 | **Surface answer + deeper loop** (the re-hook) | What does that do to you? | "That's the point. Now your hands are on it. But touching it isn't the trick." |
| 6 | **The mechanism as something happening to you.** Plain words, no names, no effect labels | | "Once it's in your hands, part of you treats it like it's already yours." |
| 7 | **Callback** that closes the hook's loop | | "That angle was never about the screen." |
| 8 | **The rule (E6):** "So if you sell anything, [one concrete move]." | | "So if you sell anything, get it into their hands before you say the price." |
| 9 | **Payoff:** the reframe that makes the rule stick | | "Because by then, they're not deciding whether to buy it. They're deciding whether to give it back." |
| 10 | **Share prompt** | | "Send this to the friend who keeps his products behind the counter." |

Optional, when there's room: one or two fast "that's why…" lines (the same trick in places the viewer has seen) between 6 and 7. They're escalation, so keep them to a line each. "Here's the trick." is optional now; a wrong guess or a pointer does the same job better.

### Worked example: the tilted laptop, before and after

**Before** (150 words, rendered 2026-10-21). It labels, then cites twice:
> Apple set every laptop in its stores to the same awkward angle. / Every morning. / In 2012, Forbes writer Carmine Gallo reported that staff tilted every screen to about seventy degrees. / … / In 2009, researchers Joann Peck and Suzanne Shu found that just touching a product makes people feel like they own it.

**After** (153 words). Same facts, same sources in the fact sheet, told as a scene:
```
It's 2012.
You walk into an Apple store.
Every laptop is tilted to the exact same angle.
Not closed.
Not quite open.
Somebody set every one of them like that before the doors opened.
You'd think it's for the glare.
It isn't.
Watch what you do next.
The angle bugs you, so you push the screen back.
That's the point.
Now your hands are on it.
But touching it isn't the trick.
It's what touching does to you.
Once it's in your hands, part of you treats it like it's already yours.
And giving back something that's yours feels like losing it.
That angle was never about the screen.
So if you sell anything, get it into their hands before you say the price.
Because by then, they're not deciding whether to buy it.
They're deciding whether to give it back.
Send this to the friend who keeps his products behind the counter.
```
Its loop map: L1 line 3 *why that angle?* → surface answer line 12, full answer line 17. L2 line 7 *then what's it for?* → line 11. L3 line 9 *what do I do?* → line 10. L4 line 13 *what does touching do?* → lines 15–16. L5 line 18 *why before the price?* → lines 19–20.

## 3. Hooks for this pillar

| Code | Type | Shape (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| **N12** ⭐ | Drop-in at the brand | "Walk into [store]. [The odd thing you'd see]." / "You're holding [product]. [The detail you never noticed]." |
| **N9** | Brand secret, on purpose | "[Brand] [does odd thing]. On purpose." / "[Product] isn't for sale. It's there to sell [the other one]." |
| **N10** | Same thing, one change | "Same [product]. [One difference]. [Different result]." |
| **N11** | Number paradox | "[$1,499] and [$1499] cost the same. One sells more." |

Always a real, named brand, a concrete number, and the viewer in front of it within three lines (N9 to N11 work as line 1 if line 2 or 3 drops "you" into the scene). The loop is "why would they do that?", and the payoff closes it.

## 4. Rules

1. **Every fact verified this session**, with a link in the fact sheet. Brand claims need a reputable source (the company, a major outlet, a book with the author named), not a marketing blog. If the reference account got a detail wrong, we get it right. **In the script, facts are told, never cited:** no "Forbes reported", no "researchers found". The source goes in the fact sheet and the Playbook entry, never the caption (a teaser).
2. **One brand, one trick.** A second brand only appears as a "that's why" example.
3. **The rule is for sellers**, concrete and doable by a one-person business this week.
4. **Kind, not manipulative.** Steal tricks that make a product feel better (touch it, hold it, price it cleanly). Flag any dark pattern as a warning, not a tip.
5. **Original words.** Same topic is fine (facts are facts); same wording, same examples or the same sign-off is not. Use our turn line and our share prompt.
6. **Length 100 to 155 words.** The checker allows it for F7 and skips the objection rule.
7. **Visuals:** the brand's product as a simple prop in the minimal kit (a laptop, a price tag, a bowl), the mechanism as one picture. Never a real logo; describe the product generically ("a silver laptop", "a white phone").

## 5. Topic leads (verify before using)
Pricing anchors and decoys, precise vs round prices, the pain of paying, scarcity badges, endowed progress cards, packaging weight, unboxing, store layouts, free samples, loss framing in subscriptions, menu design.
