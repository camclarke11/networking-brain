# 04 — The Lead Engine: how a script keeps them watching

A script is a chain of questions. The hook opens the first one. Every few lines the viewer gets an answer **and a new question at the same moment**, so there is never a line where it's safe to scroll. Callaway calls this the curiosity loop: see something, wonder, get context, wonder again. Elliott's best stories never stop doing it. (Sources and numbers: `11-storytelling-principles.md`, `00-what-performs.md` §3b and §4.)

This works the same for a Move (F1 to F6) and a Brand X-ray (F7, skeleton in `15-brand-xray.md`). The example lines below come from our first niche (networking); swap in any business moment.

---

## 1. Three laws

1. **The relay.** Never close a loop until the next one is open. The line that answers a question should also raise one. "Now your hands are on it." answers *what do you do?* and raises *so what?* A line that answers and asks nothing hands the viewer the exit.
2. **The big answer comes last.** The hook's main question is paid off in the last 20%, by the payoff line. Smaller answers come earlier, and each one opens a deeper question ("But touching it isn't the trick. It's what touching does to you."). If the hook's question is fully answered by the middle, the second half is a lecture.
3. **Show, never explain.** Never stop the story to tell the viewer what it means. No "researchers found", no "psychologists call it", no named effects, no moral ("The lesson here is…"). The meaning comes out of what happens to the viewer in the scene. Facts are verified and told as things that happened (`07-research-playbook.md` §3).

## 2. The lead toolkit

A turn or a pull every 2 to 4 lines. Use at least three different devices per script, and never the same one twice in a row. Write your own words in these shapes.

| Device | What it does | Shape (ours) |
|---|---|---|
| **Forward pointer** | Promises a specific thing that hasn't happened yet | "And the line that gets you the intro hasn't come up yet." |
| **Wrong guess** | Says the obvious explanation, then kills it | "You'd think it's for the glare. It isn't." |
| **Turn** | BUT, UNTIL, EXCEPT or INSTEAD flips the last line | "Until he asks what you do." |
| **Escalation** | Each beat raises the number or the stakes | "Ten seconds. Then he checks his phone. Then he's gone." |
| **The withheld thing** | Says something exists, not what it is | "There's one question that fixes this." |
| **Asked question** | Says the viewer's question out loud, answered within 4 lines. Once per script | "So why would Apple want that?" |
| **Most people stop here** | Splits the viewer from the crowd before the second half | "Most people stop there. Don't." |
| **Clock** | Puts time pressure on the scene | "You've got about ten seconds before he moves on." |
| **Objection** | The viewer's doubt in their words, then the answer | "You're thinking, won't that be weird? It's the opposite." |

| **Restate the premise** | One line just before the payoff that reminds the viewer what they're waiting for. People arrive mid-video from the feed (`16-short-form-guide.md` B5) | "So why would Apple want your hands on it?" |
| **The skeptic (B-plot)** | A second voice that doubts it or does it wrong, so there's tension (B6) | "Jay thinks that's mad. Jay still charges by the hour." |

A run of plain lines is fine **only if each one escalates** (a bigger number, a worse moment). Five flat lines of context in a row is where viewers leave. The guide's rule of thumb: a rehook at least every 6 to 10 seconds, which is about every 3 to 5 lines at our pace.

## 3. The beats (the Move, F1 to F6)

Target **130 to 160 words** (about 45 to 55 seconds). Hard ceiling 190.

| # | Beat | % | Opens | Closes | Shape (ours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead hook** | 0–10% | The main question | | "When you know nobody at an event, find this person first." `03-hooks.md` |
| 2 | **Scene + the problem** | 10–25% | How do I get out of this? | Where are we? | "You're by the drinks. Every circle's closed. You pretend to check your phone." |
| 3 | **Wrong move or wrong guess** | 20–30% | Then what works? | | "So you go for the CEO. He's polite for ten seconds. Then he's gone." |
| 4 | **The move, shown** | 30–50% | Will that work for me? | What works? | The exact words, and the other person's reaction. Name the tool if it's reusable (`06-formats.md`). |
| 5 | **Objection** | ~50% | | Will it work for me? | "You're thinking, why would he bother? Because introducing people is his favorite thing." |
| 6 | **Re-hook + second half** | 55–80% | What's the rest of it? | It, by the end of the beat | "But most people stop there. Give him a job." |
| 7 | **Payoff** | 85–95% | | The main question | Calls back to the hook's picture or words. `05-endings-and-ctas.md` |
| 8 | **Share prompt** | last line | | | "Send this to whoever's coming with you." |

There is **no proof beat**. If the move needs to feel safe, show it working in the scene: the other person's face, the reply that comes back, what happens next week.

Order is flexible in the middle (the objection can come before the move). Hook first, payoff and share last, always.

## 4. The loop map (Step 6, and `## LOOP MAP` in the output)

Before drafting, list the loops: the line that opens each one, the question in the viewer's head, and the line that closes it.

```
L1  line 1   Who is "this person"?                 → closed line 6 (the connector), deepened line 12, paid off line 18
L2  line 3   How do you get out of the corner?     → closed line 6
L3  line 7   Why would he help you?                → closed line 9
L4  line 10  What's the half most people skip?     → closed line 14
```

The map passes when:
- L1 opens in the hook (the first three lines) and its full answer is in the last 20%.
- Every line that closes a loop (except the payoff) opens the next one, or the next loop opens on the line after.
- No stretch of more than about 4 lines sits outside an open loop.
- No loop stays open more than about 8 lines without progress (except L1).

## 5. But / so, never "and then" or "also"

- Between every two beats you should be able to write **BUT** or **SO** (Callaway's "therefore"). If you can only write "and" or "also", you have a list. Cut one item or turn it into a problem.
- Tip lists ("Here are 5 ways…") are banned in the spoken script. Put the extra steps in the script's Playbook entry.
- The reference breakouts use "But most people stop there." / "But a quick hey won't work." exactly where a list would have gone.

## 6. Show it on screen

The video is animated, so **the move should happen on screen**:
- Not "ask about their goals". Instead: "You ask, where do you want all this to go? Watch his face change."
- Not "be memorable". Instead: the specific line, and the other person's reaction.
Every beat 2 to 7 should be something you can picture. If a line is pure explanation, rewrite it as something happening.

## 7. One move per video

One tool, one person, one line, one test. If the idea has five parts, the video teaches one or two and the Playbook entry holds the rest. The FORD video works because the acronym makes four parts feel like one thing.

## 8. The payoff closes the loop

Write the payoff line **before** the hook (`05-endings-and-ctas.md`). Then check:
- Does the last line answer the hook's question?
- Does it call back to a word or a picture from line 1, so the replay loops?
- Would someone screenshot or repeat it?
- Does it describe the viewer **after** using the move (identity), not just the move?
