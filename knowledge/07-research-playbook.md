# 07 — Research Playbook

This account's credibility rests on two things: advice that works when someone tries it, and facts that are right. A comment that says "that's not what the study found" can sink a video in this niche.

**Research everything, cite nothing in the script.** Every claim is checked this session and sourced in the fact sheet. The spoken script tells it as what happened, inside a scene, because narrated citations cost us retention (`00-what-performs.md` §4). See §3.

**Never write a study from memory alone.** Search for it, open a reputable source (the paper, the journal page, a university write-up, or solid journalism about it), and confirm the year, the researchers, what they did and what happened.

---

## 1. The fact sheet (build before drafting)

| Item | Why | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The situation** | The hook and scene live here | A founder at their first industry mixer, knowing nobody |
| **A vs B** | The contrast that drives the hook | Asking "what do you do?" vs asking what they're working on |
| **The move, word for word** | The product of the video | The exact opener, DM or follow-up text |
| **Why it works** | One line, plain words | People enjoy talking about themselves more than hearing about you |
| **The facts behind it** | Keeps us right. Never narrated as a citation | Tamir and Mitchell (2012): talking about yourself activates reward areas of the brain. In the script: "Watch his face when he gets to talk about it." |
| **The objection** | Beat 4 | "Won't that make me look needy?" |
| **The limits** | Honesty, and a caveat for the Playbook entry | Works for small asks, not for getting someone to invest |
| **The payoff** | Your last line | Before → after, or a reframe |

## 2. Where to look
1. **The paper itself** or its journal abstract (Google Scholar, the publisher, PubMed, PsyArXiv).
2. **University press releases and reputable science journalism** (Harvard Business Review, Scientific American, BPS Research Digest, The Atlantic, NYT, BBC).
3. **Books by the researchers** (Cialdini, Epley, Grant, Gilovich) for context, then check the original study.
4. **Wikipedia** only to find the citation. Never the only source.
5. **Reddit, X, TikTok:** for angles and what people struggle with. Never for facts.

## 3. Turning a fact into a scene (never a citation)
- **Put the viewer where the people in the study were.** Not "In 2011, researchers gave fifty people yogurt in three bowls." Instead: "You're handed the same yogurt in three bowls. They look identical. One's heavier. That's the one you'd swear tastes richer."
- **Or show it in the business moment.** Not "Studies show people like those who ask follow-up questions." Instead: "You ask one more question about his answer. Watch him lean in."
- **Brand facts are said as fact.** Not "Forbes reported that staff tilt the screens." Instead: "Somebody set every one of them like that before the doors opened."
- **Never claim more than the result.** Use its real scale words ("more", "nearly doubled", "rated higher"). Never invent a percentage. A study about strangers on a train is not a study about investors.
- **If the effect is debated or failed to replicate, skip it.** We can't hedge with "some research suggests" when we don't name research.
- **The fact sheet carries the citation** (year, researchers, what they did, what happened, link), and the Playbook entry credits it. The spoken script never does, and neither does the caption (it's a teaser). `tools/check_script.py` fails "researchers", "a study", "studies", "scientists", "psychologists", "according to" and similar in the SCRIPT.

## 4. Advice must be safe and kind
- No manipulation framing. The moves are about being more interesting, more useful and more memorable, not about tricking people.
- Nothing that would get someone thrown out of an event, blocked or refunded: no fake familiarity, no lying about who you know, no fake scarcity, no hidden fees, no pressure.
- It works for a one-person business: no ad budget, no team, no special software required.
- Moves should still work for someone shy. If it only works for a natural, it's off-brand.

## 5. Finding the angle
- **Zoom in on one moment:** not "sales", but "the five seconds after you say your price"; not "networking", but "the first 30 seconds after someone says their name".
- **Find the brand that already does it:** most good small-business moves have a famous brand version (a Brand X-ray), and most brand tricks have a one-person version (a Move). Look for both.
- **Find what everyone does:** the default line or habit is the A in your A vs B.
- **Flip a piece of standard advice:** "Ask more questions" → "Questions feel like an interview, so go first."
- **Steal from other fields, not other creators:** sales, hospitality, improv, negotiation, journalism (how reporters get busy people to talk).
- **The "this week" test:** can the viewer try it within seven days? If not, find the version they can.

## 6. Study bank (starting points, verify before every use)

These are well-known studies that fit the niche. Details are summaries to orient research, **not** facts to paste. Confirm each one before it goes in a script. Studies @theoryofbehaviour has already used are marked; we can use the same science, but never in their framing or with their example.

| Study | One-line finding | Business moment it fits (these first ones are networking: Get clients) | Used by them? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jecker and Landy (1969), Ben Franklin effect | People asked for a favor ended up liking the asker more | Asking someone you admire for a small favor | Yes |
| Aronson and Linder (1965), gain-loss effect | People liked someone more when their opinion moved from negative to positive | Light disagreement before agreeing | Yes |
| Brooks, Gino and Schweitzer (2015) | Asking for advice made people seem more competent | Cold DMs to busy people | Yes |
| Zhao and Epley (2021) | People underestimate how good a compliment makes the receiver feel | Openers, follow-ups | Yes |
| Nisbett and Wilson (1977), halo effect | Warmth changed how students rated a lecturer's looks and accent | First 30 seconds | Yes |
| Davis and Knowles (1999), disrupt-then-reframe | An odd phrasing then a reason increased sales | Making an ask | Yes |
| Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom and Clark (2018), the liking gap | After talking, people underestimate how much the other person liked them | Following up after an event | No |
| Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino (2017) | Asking more questions, especially follow-up questions, increased liking | Keeping a conversation going | No |
| Tamir and Mitchell (2012) | Talking about yourself activates reward regions; people gave up money to do it | Why letting them talk works | No |
| Epley and Schroeder (2014) | Commuters who talked to a stranger had a better journey than they predicted | Talking to strangers at all | No |
| Flynn and Lake (2008) | People underestimate how often others will agree to help when asked | Asking for an intro | No |
| Kumar and Epley (2018) | Senders underestimate how much a thank-you note means to the receiver | The follow-up message | No |
| Liu, Rim, Min and Min (2022) | People underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to | Reconnecting with an old contact | No |
| Levin, Walter and Murnighan (2011) | Reconnecting with dormant ties gave more useful, novel advice | Messaging people you've lost touch with | No |
| Granovetter (1973), strength of weak ties | Acquaintances pass on more new opportunities than close friends | Why the event matters | No |
| Rajkumar et al. (2022), LinkedIn experiment | Moderately weak ties were most helpful for job moves | Who to keep in touch with | No |
| Aronson, Willerman and Floyd (1966), pratfall effect | A competent person who makes a small blunder can become more likable | Admitting a small mistake | No |
| Bruk, Scholl and Bless (2018), beautiful mess effect | People see others' vulnerability more positively than their own | The small confession opener | No |
| Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978), "because" | Giving a reason, even a thin one, raised compliance with a small request | Asking for a small favor | No |
| Freedman and Fraser (1966), foot in the door | Agreeing to a small request made people more likely to agree to a bigger one | Starting with a tiny ask | No |
| Regan (1971), reciprocity | People who got a free drink bought more raffle tickets from the giver | Giving before asking | No |
| Burger et al. (2004), incidental similarity | A shared small detail (like a birthday) raised compliance | Finding something in common | No |
| Moreland and Beach (1992), mere exposure | Students rated a classmate who attended more often as more likable | Going to the same event repeatedly | No |

| Kahneman et al. (1993), peak-end rule | People judge an experience by its peak and its end | Leaving a conversation well | No |
| Walter, Levin and Murnighan (2015), reconnection choices | The most valuable dormant ties offered novelty (people they'd spent less time with), but people preferred reconnecting with old close ties | Who to message first when reconnecting | No |
| Aron et al. (1997), fast friends | Gradually deeper questions built closeness between strangers | Going one level deeper | No |
| Peck and Shu (2009), mere touch | Merely touching an object increases perceived ownership, which affects how much people value it | F7: let customers touch it first (Apple's tilted laptops) | No (@decoyreport yes). Verified 2026-10-21 |
