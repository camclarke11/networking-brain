# Short-Form Video Script Guide

This skill tells you how to write scripts for vertical short-form video: YouTube Shorts, TikTok and Instagram Reels. It is built from what successful short-form creators and strategists (Jenny Hoyos, Kallaway, Paddy Galloway, Adam Mosseri) have said publicly about why videos get views.

Read the whole file before writing. Part 1 explains how distribution works. Part 2 turns that into principles. Parts 3–8 are the procedure, output format, checklist, inputs, worked example and failure modes.

---

## Part 1 — How short-form distribution actually works

Every principle in this guide follows from one fact. **Platforms show a new video to a small test audience, watch what those viewers do, and then either widen or stop distribution.** A script's job is to produce good behaviour in that test audience.

Three behaviours matter most.

### 1. Did they stop scrolling? (the hook)
- YouTube Studio reports this as **"Viewed vs. Swiped Away" (VVSA)**: the share of feed impressions where the viewer watched rather than swiped.
- Strategist **Paddy Galloway** analysed 5,400 Shorts across 33 channels (about 3.3B views) and treated VVSA as the key hook metric. A secondary write-up of his work reports that Shorts with roughly 70–90% VVSA performed best, and reach collapsed below about 60%. Treat those thresholds as directional, not exact.
- Implication: **if the first 1–3 seconds fail, nothing after them is ever seen.** The hook is a gate, not an ornament.

### 2. Did they keep watching? (retention / average view duration)
- On Instagram, **Adam Mosseri** (Head of Instagram) has publicly named watch time as one of the three most important ranking signals.
- In Galloway's 2023 study, Shorts with an average view duration above 50 seconds averaged about 4.1M views. Absolute seconds watched mattered, not just percentage.
- Creator **Jenny Hoyos** has averaged roughly 10M+ views per Short and has 7M+ subscribers. She treats 90% retention as good and 95%+ as excellent.

### 3. Did they rewatch or share? (the multiplier)
- **Rewatches:** if average percentage viewed exceeds 100%, people are looping the video. Hoyos says Shorts under 30 seconds need over 100% retention to compete. One Minecraft creator raised percentage viewed to nearly 200% by building seamless loops.
- **Shares:** Mosseri has said likes matter more for reaching existing followers, while **sends per reach** (sharing a Reel by DM) matter more for reaching non-followers.

### Platform notes
- **YouTube Shorts:** the hook is judged by VVSA. Watch time and loops drive expansion. Shorts can now be up to 3 minutes, but most viral examples studied here are under 60 seconds. YouTube has announced a Studio feature to upload up to three versions of a Short and compare them on watch time.
- **Instagram Reels:** watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach. Instagram has said it shows less of low-resolution or watermarked Reels, muted Reels, Reels with borders, and Reels that are mostly text. Never script a TikTok-watermarked repost.
- **TikTok:** the same core loop applies: test audience, then hook, completion, rewatch and share. Pacing expectations are fast.

## Part 2 — Principles (with the evidence behind each)

### A. The hook

**A1. Design the first frame as if it were a thumbnail.**
Hoyos sketches thumbnails to brainstorm hooks and treats each Short as if it were a long-form video with a thumbnail. Before a word is heard, the frozen first frame should make someone curious.
*Agent rule:* always specify the exact first frame. If you can't describe a frame that works on mute, the hook isn't done.

**A2. Hook in 3 seconds or less, and start mid-action.**
Hoyos keeps hooks to 3 seconds or less. She has said you have about one second to hook someone on Shorts. In her teardowns she skips any preamble and explains while doing. She also never says aloud what the visuals already show: on a frozen-yogurt video she didn't need to say "frozen yogurt."
*Agent rule:* no greetings, no "so today…", no "in this video…". The first spoken words are the premise.

**A3. Align the three hooks.**
Creator **Kallaway** (1B+ views, short-form storytelling educator) teaches the "triple hook". The **visual hook** (what's shown), **spoken hook** (what's said) and **text hook** (on-screen words) must all point at the same single idea. When they diverge, the viewer spends effort reconciling them and swipes. He also mentions a fourth layer: the first line of the caption.
*Agent rule:* write all three for the opening beat and check they agree.

**A4. One subject, one question.**
Kallaway's hook ingredients are a clear **subject**, real **stakes**, **speed** and **clarity**. He says the topic must be obvious within 1–2 seconds. A hook should raise exactly one question the viewer wants answered.

**A5. Borrow recognition.**
In some hooks Hoyos deliberately kept her face out of the opening and showed a famous brand or person instead (Chick-fil-A, Logan Paul). Recognisable things stop the scroll faster than an unknown face.
*Agent rule:* if the topic involves a well-known brand, place, product or person, put it in the first frame.

**A6. Match the promise.**
A hook that oversells what the video delivers wins the swipe but loses retention and satisfaction. The payoff must satisfy what the hook promised. Hoyos frames great videos as a strong closing paying off the hook's promise.

### B. The body

**B1. Write at about a 5th-grade reading level.**
Hoyos analysed viral Shorts and found most sat at a 5th-grade reading level or below. One write-up reports most MrBeast Shorts test at about 1st-grade level.
*Agent rule:* short words, short sentences, no jargon. If a term is unavoidable, show it instead of explaining it.

**B2. Set the stakes immediately after the hook.**
In the frozen-yogurt teardown, the second line establishes conflict: a normal cup would cost about $20, and she only has $1. In the chicken sandwich video: the sandwich is great, *but* she won't pay $6, *so* she'll make it for $1 and compare.
*Agent rule:* beat 2 = why this is hard, risky, surprising or matters.

**B3. Connect beats with "but" and "so", never "and then".**
Hoyos uses "but" and "so" to move a story. Each beat should either complicate the last one (*but*) or follow from it (*so*). A list of "and then" beats feels flat.

**B4. Rehook repeatedly.**
Kallaway structures videos as: hook → a "dance" of setup and rehook, repeated → an ending that hooks again so people share. A rehook is a line that reopens curiosity. Examples: "But that's not the weird part." "And that's when it went wrong." "Here's the problem."
*Agent rule:* at least one rehook every 6–10 seconds.

**B5. Restate the premise before the payoff.**
Just before the reveal, Hoyos repeats the question ("14 flavors — is it under a dollar?"). Viewers arrive from a random feed position and need reminding of what they're waiting for. It raises investment in the answer.

**B6. Add conflict and a second voice where possible.**
Hoyos uses her mum's sceptical reactions as a "B plot" to add tension. She also adds a deliberate "mistake" and a drama beat in the middle. If everything goes smoothly, nobody cares.

**B7. Vary rhythm.**
Kallaway deliberately mixes short and long sentences, and fast and slow delivery, to avoid monotony. He also matches the pacing native to each platform; slow podcast-style clips rarely work on TikTok or Reels.

**B8. Keep a visual progress marker.**
Hoyos shows on-screen reminders of the concept and progress (a $1 bill on the cup, a flavour count). Give viewers something visible that tracks toward the payoff.

### C. The ending

**C1. End abruptly on the high note.**
Hoyos cuts immediately after the payoff line. Lingering after the high point kills rewatches. In one Short she found a retention drop from about 70% to 45% in the final second. She deleted that second, retention rose to about 88%, and the video reached about 18M views.
*Agent rule:* the last spoken line is the payoff or the loop line. No "thanks for watching", no outro.

**C2. Put the call to action in the middle, at peak attention.**
Hoyos places CTAs mid-video and keeps them subtle (her mum quipping about "all this for one subscriber"). Many viewers leave right after the payoff, and an end CTA blocks rewatches.
*Agent rule:* one CTA, placed around 40–60% through, phrased as part of the story ("follow for part two").

**C3. Engineer the loop.**
Write the **last line and the first line together**. Hoyos develops the hook and the last line early. Common loop forms:
- The last sentence flows grammatically into the first ("…go ask Google for everything it knows about you." → "I asked Google for everything it knows about me.").
- The last visual matches the first frame.
- The ending poses or reframes the question the opening asks.

**C4. Hook the share.**
Kallaway uses the ending to prompt sharing. Endings that hand viewers something to do, test, send or argue about earn sends, which Instagram weights for reaching non-followers.

### D. Length

- Hoyos's sweet spot is about **34 seconds**.
- Galloway's 2023 data showed longer Shorts (50–60s) averaging more views. That was driven by higher average view duration in seconds, and predates the 3-minute limit.
- The reconciliation: **length is not the target; seconds watched is.** Make the video as long as the story can hold attention, and no longer.
- *Agent default:* 25–45 seconds (about 70–130 spoken words at a brisk pace). Go to 50–60s only if every beat rehooks. Go under 25s only for loop-built or single-reveal ideas.

### E. Idea selection (the stage before scripting)

- Hoyos keeps about 1,000 ideas but only produces about 10. She will change the whole video idea to get a stronger hook.
- Pick ideas with a broad audience, a single clear question, a visual first frame and a satisfying payoff.
- Kallaway's lens: take a familiar topic and approach it from an angle only this creator can give. For example, he covered Taylor Swift at the Super Bowl through its financial impact on the NFL.

### F. Testing

- Hoyos tests multiple intros (up to about 10) and multiple versions of the same story.
- *Agent rule:* always deliver at least 3 alternative hooks that keep the rest of the script identical, so they can be A/B tested.

## Part 3 — Procedure: how to write a script

Follow these steps in order.

1. **Collect inputs** (Part 6). If something critical is missing and you can't ask, make a reasonable choice and state the assumption at the top.
2. **Write the premise in one sentence** a 10-year-old would understand. If you can't, the idea isn't ready.
3. **Name the single question** the viewer will wait for (e.g. "Is it under a dollar?" or "What does Google actually have on me?").
4. **Write the payoff line and the loop line first** (C1, C3).
5. **Design the opening frame**, then the text hook and spoken hook. Check alignment (A1–A4).
6. **Write the stakes beat** (B2).
7. **Write the body beats** linked by but/so, with a rehook every 6–10s (B3–B4) and the CTA at 40–60% (C2).
8. **Restate the premise** just before the payoff (B5).
9. **Edit for reading level and rhythm.** Cut every word that the visuals already say. Mix sentence lengths.
10. **Time it** at about 2.8–3 spoken words per second. Adjust to the target length.
11. **Write 3 alternative hooks** for testing.
12. **Run the checklist** (Part 5) and fix every failure before returning.

### Honesty rules
- Never invent facts, statistics, prices or personal experiences. When the script depends on something only the creator knows (their numbers, what they found, their reaction), use a **[bracketed placeholder]** with a note on what goes there.
- The hook must be true. Curiosity is fine; misleading claims are not. They also hurt retention when the payoff disappoints.

## Part 4 — Output format

Return scripts in this structure:

```
TITLE: <working title>
LENGTH: ~<seconds>s · ~<words> spoken words
PREMISE: <one sentence>
QUESTION: <the single question viewers wait for>
ASSUMPTIONS: <only if you had to guess>

[0–2s] HOOK
  VISUAL: <exact first frame; must work on mute>
  TEXT:   <on-screen text, ≤6 words>
  VO:     <spoken line>

[2–5s] STAKES
  VISUAL: ...
  VO:     ...

[5–xs] <BEAT NAME>
  VISUAL: ...
  TEXT:   <optional>
  VO:     ...
  (mark rehooks with ↺ and the CTA with ★)

[xx–end] PAYOFF / LOOP
  VISUAL: ... → HARD CUT
  VO:     <final line — flows into the opening line>

ALT HOOKS (rest of script unchanged):
  1. ...
  2. ...
  3. ...

PRODUCTION NOTES: <only what's essential: B-roll needed, real details to capture>
```

## Part 5 — Pre-delivery checklist (all must pass)

**Hook**
- [ ] First frame described and understandable on mute
- [ ] Topic clear within 2 seconds; hook ≤3 seconds
- [ ] Visual, text and spoken hooks all point at the same single idea
- [ ] Raises exactly one question
- [ ] Contains no greeting, intro or channel name
- [ ] Doesn't say aloud what the visual already shows

**Body**
- [ ] Stakes or conflict established by about 5 seconds
- [ ] Beats linked by but/so (no "and then" chains)
- [ ] A rehook at least every 6–10 seconds
- [ ] Premise restated just before the payoff
- [ ] About 5th-grade reading level; no unexplained jargon
- [ ] Sentence length varies

**Ending**
- [ ] Exactly one CTA, placed mid-video, phrased as part of the story
- [ ] Payoff fully satisfies the hook's promise
- [ ] Ends immediately on the payoff or loop line with no outro
- [ ] Last line or frame loops into the first

**Integrity and format**
- [ ] No invented facts; creator-specific details are [placeholders]
- [ ] Length within target; word count matches about 3 words per second
- [ ] 3 alternative hooks provided
- [ ] Nothing that would require a watermark, border or mostly-text frame on Reels

## Part 6 — Inputs to gather

**Essential**
- Topic or idea
- Platform(s)
- Creator's niche and credibility (why *they* are the one telling this)
- What the creator can actually film (face to camera, screen recording, B-roll, a second person)

**Helpful**
- Target length
- Audience (broad or niche)
- Real details, numbers or results the creator has
- Recurring series or a follow-up video to tease in the CTA
- Examples of the creator's past hits and flops, plus their VVSA and retention if available

If only a topic is given: assume a broad audience, YouTube Shorts plus Reels, face to camera plus screen or B-roll, and about 35 seconds. State those assumptions.

## Part 7 — Worked example

```
TITLE: Everything Google knows about me
LENGTH: ~38s · ~110 words
PREMISE: I downloaded every bit of data Google holds on me and opened it.
QUESTION: What does Google actually have on me?

[0–2s] HOOK
  VISUAL: Phone showing the Google Takeout download completing, with a
          huge file size visible; creator's face reacting in corner
  TEXT:   EVERYTHING GOOGLE KNOWS ABOUT ME
  VO:     "I asked Google for everything it knows about me."

[2–5s] STAKES
  VISUAL: Zoom on file size
  VO:     "It sent me [38] gigabytes. And in the UK, they have to."

[5–10s] TRANSITION ↺
  VISUAL: Unzip; scroll an endless folder list
  TEXT:   [52] FOLDERS
  VO:     "So I opened it. Most of it was boring. But then I found this."

[10–17s] BEAT 1
  VISUAL: Search history file scrolling, stop on one line
  VO:     "Every search I've made since [2016]. Including [real, funny,
          harmless search]."

[17–22s] REHOOK + CTA ↺ ★
  VISUAL: Cut to face
  VO:     "But that's not the weird part. I'm doing Instagram's file next,
          so follow for that."

[22–31s] BEAT 2
  VISUAL: Open [most surprising real folder]
  VO:     "It knows [real stat]. It thinks I'm interested in [something
          wildly wrong]."

[31–38s] PAYOFF / LOOP
  VISUAL: Fast cuts, face ↔ screen → HARD CUT
  VO:     "It's called Google Takeout. Takes two minutes. Go ask Google for
          everything it knows about you."
          (loops into: "I asked Google for everything it knows about me.")

ALT HOOKS:
  1. "Google sent me a [38] gigabyte file about myself."
  2. "This is everything Google has on me." (open on folder already scrolling)
  3. "You can legally make Google send you everything it has on you."

PRODUCTION NOTES: Film your real export; the specific real details are
the point. Choose the funniest search and the most wrong ad interest.
```

**Why it passes:**
- The opening pairs a recognisable brand with a big number, and all three hooks agree.
- The question is single and obvious.
- Beats are linked by but/so, with rehooks at about 5s and 17s.
- The CTA is mid-video and framed as a follow-up.
- It ends on the instruction, which loops grammatically into the opening line.

## Part 8 — Common failure modes

| Failure | Symptom in analytics | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Slow or verbal-only opening | Low VVSA | Start mid-action with a visual first frame |
| Misaligned hooks | Low VVSA | Make text, visual and voice say one thing |
| Hook oversells | OK VVSA, steep early drop | Make the promise match the payoff |
| Flat middle ("and then…") | Gradual retention slide | Add but/so conflict and rehooks |
| Explaining what's visible | Mid-video drop | Cut redundant narration |
| Lingering ending or outro | Sharp drop in final seconds; no loops | Hard cut on the payoff |
| CTA at the end | Low rewatch | Move it mid-video |
| Too long for the story | Low % viewed | Cut about 30% of words; remove weakest beat |

## Evidence quality and caveats

- Much of this comes from creators' own accounts and third-party write-ups of their interviews, not from platform documentation. Strong patterns (hook importance, abrupt endings, rehooks) recur across independent creators. Precise numbers (VVSA thresholds, reading levels, ideal length) are directional.
- Galloway's study is from 2023, before Shorts could run 3 minutes and before YouTube changed how Shorts views are counted in 2025. Use its *relative* findings (seconds watched matters), not its exact figures.
- Platform signals change. Re-check current guidance from YouTube, Instagram (Mosseri) and TikTok periodically.

## How this guide fits our brain (read this)

The guide and our rules agree on almost everything: a hook that leads (`03-hooks.md`), but/so instead of "and then", rehooks (`04-lesson-engine.md`), writing the payoff and the loop first, no invented facts. Where they differ, **our data wins** (`00-what-performs.md`), and the difference is written down here so nobody has to guess.

### Where our data overrides the guide
| The guide says | We do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Default length 25 to 45 seconds (70 to 130 words) | **130 to 160 words** (45 to 55s) for a Move, **100 to 155** for a Brand X-ray | Our reference winners sit there: @theoryofbehaviour's breakouts are about 145 to 155 words and @decoyreport's are 100 to 155. The guide itself says length isn't the target; seconds watched is. If a script can't rehook every few seconds at that length, cut it. |
| One CTA mid-video, at 40 to 60% | **The share prompt goes last** ("Send this to the friend who…") while the Playbook is coming soon | Both @theoryofbehaviour breakouts end on a share prompt, and shares reach non-followers (the guide's own point about sends). When the Playbook goes live, **test** the guide's mid-video placement for the Playbook CTA against the end placement (`05-endings-and-ctas.md`). |
| Output format (Part 4) with timestamps | `templates/script-output.md`, and never seconds in the storyboard | Audio first: times come from the recorded voice (`12-visual-storyboard.md` §6). Use the guide's format only as a thinking aid. |
| Start mid-action; a greeting is never allowed | Same, and we add: no citations either | Narrated research costs us retention (`00-what-performs.md` §4). |

### What we've adopted from it (now in the rules)
- **The triple hook (A3):** frame one, the on-screen `big_text` and the spoken line 1 point at the same single idea, and the cover title matches. The caption is the fourth layer, but ours is a teaser by design (`05-endings-and-ctas.md`). Checklist item.
- **Borrow recognition (A5):** a famous brand, product or place goes in the first frame. That's the Brand X-ray's whole engine.
- **Reading level (B1):** about a **5th-grade** reading level (it was 6th). The checker estimates it.
- **Restate the premise before the payoff (B5):** a line just before the final answer that reminds viewers what they're waiting for ("So why would Apple want your hands on it?"). Added to `04-lesson-engine.md`.
- **A second voice (B6):** a skeptic or a B-plot adds tension, like Jay doing it wrong or a client who doesn't buy it. Use one when it fits.
- **A visual progress marker (B8):** something on screen that tracks toward the payoff (a tag that grows, a path that draws, steps that light). It goes in the storyboard (`12-visual-storyboard.md`).
- **End on the high note, hard cut (C1):** the last line is the payoff or the share prompt, with no outro and no lingering frame. The video's end-fade starts straight after the last word.
- **Three alternative hooks (F):** already in HOOK OPTIONS. Keep them A/B-ready: the rest of the script stays identical.
- **Timing:** about 2.8 to 3 words per second spoken. `config.json` → `words_per_minute` (175) is our measured pace.
- **What the platforms reward (Part 1):** the hook decides viewed-vs-swiped, watch time decides reach, and sends per reach decide non-follower reach on Instagram. Every rule above serves one of those three.
