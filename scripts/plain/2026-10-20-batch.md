## 1. The person standing alone

Ten minutes into an event and everyone's already in a group?
Go find the one person who isn't.

You're by the drinks, fake scrolling your phone.
Every circle's closed.

But look again.
Someone else is doing exactly what you're doing.
Say his name's Sam.
Lanyard on, full drink, eyes on the door.
He just got here before his friends.

So walk over and say, I don't know a single person here. You?

You're thinking, he'll think I'm a loser.
He's praying someone like you saves him.

In 2014, researchers had Chicago commuters talk to strangers on the train.
Most people predicted silence would be nicer.
The ones who talked had the better ride.

But don't stop at two.
Next person standing alone, you both wave him over.
Now you're the group.

You walked in waiting to be saved.
You walked out with your own group.

Send this to the friend who hides in the bathroom at events.

Don't.
Reply, no stress, see you at the next one.

Thursday is where they actually happen.

## 4. The echo

The easiest way to keep someone talking is three words long.

Dev's telling you about his agency.
You're nodding, planning your next question.
He can tell.

So stop planning and use the echo.
Repeat his last few words back, as a question.
Then stop talking.

He says, we nearly went under last year.
You say, nearly went under?
And he tells you the whole story.

You're thinking, won't he notice I'm copying him?

In 2003, researchers had a waitress repeat some customers' orders back, word for word.
Those tables tipped her more.

It doesn't sound like copying.
It sounds like you're listening.

But most people echo the wrong words.
You skip the boring bit.
You echo the bit with feeling in it.
Went under, not last year.
That's where his real story is.

You'll barely say ten words.
He'll still call you the best chat of the night.

Send this to the friend who talks over everyone at dinner.

## 5. The liking gap

You think that conversation went badly.
It didn't.

You're walking home from the event.
Replaying the bit where you forgot his name.
The joke that died.
The weird goodbye.
So you don't text him.
Big mistake.

In 2018, psychologists had strangers chat, then guess how much the other person liked them.
They kept guessing low.
Their partners liked them more than they thought.
Researchers call it the liking gap.

You're thinking, no, I really was awkward.
Maybe.
But you were too busy judging yourself to notice him enjoying it.

You're grading yourself on the bloopers.
He's grading you on the highlights.
So your review of that chat doesn't count.
His does.

Text him anyway.
Good chatting last night, you've got me rethinking my pricing.
That's it.

The guy who sends it gets a second conversation.
The guy who doesn't never finds out it went fine.

You're twenty minutes in with Marco.

In 1993, psychologists had people hold a hand in painfully cold water.

The ending mattered more than the length.

Let me grab you again later, I promised myself three new people tonight.

You're thinking, won't leaving a good chat feel rude?

Now he watches you go, still smiling.
Fizzling out makes you forgettable.
Leaving on a laugh makes him come find you later.

## 10. Wait for the laugh

Here's how to join a group that's already talking, without hovering.

You spot three people mid-conversation.
You drift closer.
And closer.
Nobody looks up.
Now you're the guy standing half a step behind a circle.

So stop waiting for an invite.
Wait for the laugh.
Right after a laugh, the circle loosens for a second.
That's your gap.

Step in and say, okay, that laugh sounded like the best story here. What did I miss?

You're thinking, isn't that interrupting?
Not in the gap.
You just told them they're the fun group.

But the next ten seconds decide if you stay.
You get in and want to talk about yourself.
Don't.
Turn to whoever was telling the story and say, go on, finish it.

Now the storyteller's on your side.
And the circle opens a little wider.

Hovering makes you the guy at the edge.
One line makes you the guy who asked for the story.
